# Introduction
When the Republic of Türkiye was established in 1923, its government decided to make the nation as homogeneous as possible. This took shape, for example, in Turkish becoming the only accepted and official language, and in the unification of all nationalities - the main minority, the Kurds, were now officially referred to as Turks. Newly born children could only be given Turkish names, and even referring to the minority as Kurds became illegal. This suppression of Kurdish identity raised tension between the state and its Kurdish population, and eventually led to the establishment of the PKK.
# Early days of the PKK
The PKK was founded in 1978 by Abdullah Öcalan, a young student who supported a Marxist ideology and sought to establish an independent Kurdish state within Turkey. Shortly after the military coup of 1980, the PKK launched what it called a Kurdish uprising, which broke into open armed conflict in 1984. The fighting led to immense tragedy: an estimated 40,000 people lost their lives, with the majority of casualties being Kurdish civilians caught between the two sides. The PKK carried out bombings in city centres and recruited children as soldiers, actions that further entrenched its designation as a terrorist organization by Turkey, the US, and the EU.
However, a turning point came when Öcalan was captured in Kenya in 1999 and imprisoned on İmralı island, where he remains to this day. Slowly, peace efforts began to take shape, eventually growing into a formal peace process lasting from 2013 to 2015. This process ultimately collapsed, however, and tensions reignited - compounded by developments in Syria, where PKK-affiliated Kurdish militias (the YPG, later regrouped under the SDF) became key partners of the US-led coalition against ISIS, a role that deepened Turkey's distrust of Kurdish forces on its southern border.

# Final ceasefire, and today's situation
The most significant change occurred recently. In 2024, the Turkish government launched an initiative focused on putting an end to the conflict. But, arguably, the most important factor was Öcalan's call for peace from imprisonment. He sent a message on 27 February, 2025, to the PKK, urging ceasefire and laying down it's arms. And on 1 March, 2025, the PKK declared a ceasefire with Turkey and on 12 May announced it would dissolute; the soldiers fighting on the PKK's side were ultimately pardoned.
Despite these achievements, meaningful change on Turkey's side has yet to come. Kurdish politicians accuse Ankara of failing to deliver the reforms promised as part of the agreement - including expanded minority rights and amnesty for former fighters, both still unresolved. While Turkey has entered an "implementation phase," concrete legislative steps have yet to follow, leaving the peace process at a fragile, uncertain stage.
